## The Hard Problem of Consciousness

David Chalmers distinguished the *easy problems* of consciousness — correlating brain states with behavior and experience — from the **hard problem**: why is there subjective experience at all? Why does information processing *feel* like something from the inside?

Science can map every neural correlate of seeing red. It cannot explain why red *looks* like anything.

Our minds are limited instruments; everything they reach will carry that limitation. But awareness can know itself *prior* to the constructions of mind — and that, some argue, is the only knowledge that is truly direct.

## Can Science Find All the Answers?

Science describes — it maps relationships between measurable phenomena. But description is not the same as explanation. Every answer generates new questions. The question *"what is absolutely true?"* may point back to experience itself: the one thing that cannot be doubted even while doubting everything else.

## Free Will

At the ultimate level, if there is no separate self, there is no self to possess free will. At the relative level, neuroscience shows the brain decides before we are conscious of that decision — the "will" is often observed, not authored. Yet we retain agency: attention and intention shape future patterns of action. The practical stance: act as if you have free will, and do the best work you can.

## Panpsychism and Solipsism

**Panpsychism**: consciousness is fundamental — not produced by matter, but a basic feature of reality. Rather than explaining how matter produces mind, the question becomes how mind produces the *appearance* of matter.

**Solipsism** in its non-egocentric form: a single consciousness conceives the appearance of many minds. From the perspective of awareness itself, no ultimately separate minds are found.
