_It is hardly an exaggeration to say that the tip of the radicle… acts like the brain of one of the lower animals._ — Charles Darwin

Plants do things that make it hard to keep believing consciousness (or even decision-making) needs a brain.

In one experiment, researchers gave a plant's roots two choices. Roots detect moisture and grow toward it, which is a kind of decision without a nervous system. When they played only the sound of water, the roots still went that way.

The dodder parasite can pick up on light wavelengths to find a host plant and grow toward it.

Plants also respond to damage, talk to each other, and remember.

When a leaf gets chewed, an electrical signal moves through the plant in seconds (not via nerves, just through the tissue), and other leaves start making bitter or toxic compounds before the insect gets there. Some ooze latex. Some toughen up. They're not just sitting there getting eaten.

Damaged plants also send out airborne chemicals. Nearby plants can pick those up. Often they don't flip on full defenses right away; they get _primed_, so if something attacks them later, they react faster and harder. Clipped sagebrush can warn wild tobacco next door. Maize that "hears" a neighbor under caterpillar attack later puts out stronger scents that attract parasitic wasps. Some of this signaling also happens underground through roots and fungal networks.

And they remember. After a drought, a heat spell, or getting chewed on, the next time that stress shows up, the response is quicker and sharper. Not a thought in a head, just the plant staying ready based on what already happened.

If responsiveness goes this far, the line between "conscious" and "not" looks a lot fuzzier than we're used to.
